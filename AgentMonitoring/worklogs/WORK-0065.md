The owner wants records readable by non-experts — our own eli5: it must beat the default eli5 plugin's output in blind A/B (omit less, read easier), hit jvns.ca retellability, and look native in the app. Enforced at save so no agent can skip it; taught at write time so no session pays for it.

## For humans

Every write-up in this project used to speak to one reader: the next AI agent. The owner asked for a second half on every record — the same events, retold so a person who was not there and does not program can follow them — plus a switch on each record page to flip between the two, and a hard rule that nothing saves without its second half.

**A rulebook was tested until it beat a rival.** The style contract, docs/HUMAN_STYLE.md, is the one document an agent reads at the moment of writing. Sixteen rounds of trials shaped it: each round, writers who had seen nothing but the document and one raw record wrote its plain half, and judges who did not know which text came from where compared it against eli5 — a popular "explain it simply" tool. The contract passed when our texts won on every test record, three judges each: they left less out and read easier.

**The saving tool now refuses a half-told record.** Whichever door a record comes through, arriving without its plain half means the save is rejected — and the rejection message itself prints the writing rules. It is like a form that hands you the instructions only when you skip the one required field: the rules cost nothing until the moment someone forgets them, and skipping cannot ship.

**Every record page grew a switch.** The app shows 에이전트/사람 buttons on each record; when the plain half exists, it is what opens first. The plain view has its own reading design — numbered story steps, a bold first line per step, the closing line lifted into a tinted box. This was checked in the real desktop window, in Korean and English, 88 checks in all.

**More than a hundred old records were retold.** The project's whole history got its plain halves written, then rewritten through thirteen judged rounds, until no record read worse than eli5's version of the same events and none claimed anything its technical half does not say.

**The standing cost fell to zero.** Instructions that every future session would have read were deleted line by line, and test agents who never saw them wrote just as well — the rejection message alone taught enough. So nothing about this system is loaded "always"; the rules arrive only in the moment of writing, and a guard script keeps the printed rules identical to the contract.

**Pictures joined, under the same honesty law.** Where a shape is cheaper drawn than said — a folder tree, a before-and-after — a record may now carry a small diagram in the app's own colours. Three records have them, and every box and label in each was traced back to a sentence of the record before the reviewer would pass it.

**A final gate tried to break the whole.** Four independent reviewers ran every automated check, drove the real desktop window, replayed the write path from nothing, and read the documentation against the product line by line. What they caught was fixed: nine event entries with impossible fingerprints (and a new check so that class can never pass quietly again), a language-button press the app dropped in its first half-second, and a front page whose copy-paste example no longer ran under the new rule.

Nothing here saves any more until it is told twice — once for the next machine, and once for the person all of it was for.

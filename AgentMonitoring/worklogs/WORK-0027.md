## What

The bug board's severity column stops abbreviating in Korean, and the two gates that could
not see it learn to look.

`SeverityBadge` built its narrow-row short form with `label.charAt(0)`. That is an English
rule wearing typography's clothes: C/H/M/L is an initialism a reader expands, and the first
syllable of a Korean word is not one. The short form is now a dictionary entry per language
— `word.sevAbbr.*`, C/H/M/L in en.ts and empty in ko.ts — read through a new
`severityAbbr()` in lib/words.ts that returns null where a language has none. A pill with no
short form renders no `.pill-abbr` and wears no `.has-abbr`, and every rule in the 760px
container query is now keyed on that class rather than on the width alone.

The column that carried the saving stops naming its own width and takes its pill's, so the
language decides what the narrow state costs: English lands on 38px with its initialism,
Korean on the 62px its word already fits inside — 8px narrower than the 70px it spends at
every other width. All four severities keep one width, so the dots still line up down the
board.

Two gates were structurally blind and are not now:

* `check:i18n` walked one 1600px viewport, where no container query fires, so the entire
  narrow layout of both boards was outside every gate in the repo. It now walks the
  reflowing screens again at 960px, the floor tauri.conf.json sets.
* Alphabet checks ask which *language* a string is in and cannot ask whether it is a
  **word**. `VOCABULARIES` now asserts that the three vocabularies the app itself authors —
  work state, bug state, severity — print a value that is in the dictionary exactly. A
  second, static check refuses any short form written in Hangul at all, because the DOM
  check reads the same dictionary and would agree with a truncation typed into it.

`scripts/win-window.ps1` gains a `resize` action, so the real WebView2 window can be driven
to its own minimum width — the thing nothing in this repo could do, which is why a reader
dragging a window found this before a gate did.

## Why

The P9 round 3 critic, quoting the reader:

> src/components/ui.tsx:45 renders {label.charAt(0)} into .pill-abbr [so] between 960px and
> 1058px — the whole bottom 99px of the window range the desktop app itself allows — the
> Korean severity column reads 치 / 높 / 보 / 낮. Three of the four are not Korean words at
> all, and the fourth is worse than that: 낮 IS a Korean word and it means *daytime*,
> printed in the column that is supposed to say low severity.

Two things had to be true for this to ship. The first is that the code comment defending the
rule ("C costs seven pixels and keeps the meaning in the glyph") was the English argument
left untranslated, and nobody re-asked it in the other language. The second is that the
saving does not exist. Measured in this app at the pill's own 11px: 치명적 33.72px,
높음/보통/낮음 22.48px each, against Critical 43.08, High 28.48, Medium 49.52, Low 25.56.
Every Korean word is narrower than the English word it replaces; 낮음 is narrower than Low,
and the widest Korean word is 16px narrower than Medium, which the app prints in full one
pixel above the threshold. The 760px rule was cut to fit "Medium". Korean was being
abbreviated for a constraint only English has, and paying for it with a wrong word.

## How

The decision lives in one place — the dictionary — and everything downstream reads it.
Putting it there rather than behind an `html[lang="ko"]` rule in the CSS means a future
language that *does* have a real short form gets it by adding four strings, and a language
that does not gets the word by leaving them empty. The reasoning and the measurements are
written into the header of lib/i18n/ko.ts beside the state-word decisions, because the next
builder will meet the 62px column before they meet this record.

Verified where the critic photographed it. `npm run tauri:dev`, the real WebView2 window
resized to a 960px client area, Korean: the row's chip reads 낮음 and the severity legend
above it reads 낮음 5 — one value, one spelling, one screen. The 전체 tab shows 낮음 / 보통 /
높음 down the column with the dots aligned. Pressing English in the sidebar footer repaints
the same window into C/H/M/L instantly and writes {"locale":"en"} to settings.json; pressing
한국어 writes it back.

Mutation-tested both halves of the new gate before trusting either. Restoring
`label.charAt(0)` fails check:i18n with 30 findings, all on `bugs … @960` — the screen and
the width that had no coverage at all. Typing "낮" into `word.sevAbbr.low` instead fails the
static check, which is the case the DOM check cannot see because it reads that same value.

Measured the crossover before and after: at viewport 960 / 1000 / 1040 / 1056 / 1058 the
board printed 낮 and at 1060 it printed 낮음; it now prints 낮음 at all six, and English
still prints L below 1060 and Low above it.

## For humans

Drag the desktop window narrow and the Korean bug board stopped printing words in the column saying how bad a bug is. Each row showed one syllable: 치, 높, 보, 낮. Three are not Korean words. The fourth is: 낮 means daytime, printed in the rows meant to say a bug is not urgent. A reader dragging a window edge found it.

**The app was cutting each word down to its first character.** In English that gives C, H, M and L, initials a reader unpacks back into the word; a Korean word's first syllable unpacks into nothing. It is like keeping the first note of a tune and expecting people to hum the rest. The short form is an entry in each language's word list now — `word.sevAbbr`, C/H/M/L in English, nothing in Korean — and where it is empty none is drawn.

**The saving it was made for does not exist in Korean.** A comment in the code defended the cut: the letter costs seven pixels and keeps the meaning. That was argued in English and never re-asked in Korean. Measured here, every Korean word in that column is narrower than the English it replaces — the widest 16 pixels under Medium, the word the cut-off was sized to fit.

**Two checks were blind by build.** `npm run check:i18n` opens the app's screens and reports wording left wrong for its reader; without one, a screen ships unreadable to the reader it was written for. It read one wide window, and this narrow layout only appears below a threshold, so nothing had seen it; it reads the desktop's narrowest width now.

**Asking which language a string is in cannot tell a word from a piece of one.** 낮 is Korean either way. The same check now demands the three word lists the app writes itself print an entry exactly, and refuses a Korean short form outright.

**Putting the fault back makes them fail.** The first-character rule restored: 30 findings, all on that board at the width nothing used to visit. The syllable 낮 typed in as a short form: refused by the second check, the case the first cannot see. In the real desktop window at its narrowest, the row reads 낮음 under a legend reading 낮음.

**The same English habit had broken a date one surface over.** A rule letting a line break at any character split `2026년 8월 18` from a lone `일` beneath it at eleven widths, every one between the two the check was reading. That rule is gone. 일 counts days and cannot stand apart from the number in front of it.

**So the check learned a third question, about line breaks rather than about alphabets.** It lays a marker over each number-and-counter pair where it is drawn and asks where that pair landed; back on two lines means the word came apart, whatever the styling allowed. Nine widths, 36 readings a run, six seconds. Put the old rule back and it fails at 1190, 1200, 1360 and 1440 — four of the widths the reviewing agent had listed.

**Pressing the language switch left one screen behind.** The page shown for an address the app has nothing at asked for its words once and then listened for nothing. So English repainted the whole app around it and left it reading 화면이 없습니다 until the reader navigated away. It listens now, the way every other screen already did; take that away again and the check names the screen twice.

**Five smaller Korean repairs rode along.** Two failure sentences wrote the particle 만 with a space before it, and two dashboard hints did the same with 에 — the file's own header calls a detached particle the trace of a broken rule. An empty-chart hint carried an English pronoun across word for word and now says the plain thing. A board's subtitle dropped the 그리고 an English list puts before its last item, which a Korean list does not take. And a project card printed bare numerals, 완료 27 · 진행 중 0, where Korean counts with a counter: 완료 27개 · 진행 중 0개.

A short form only works where the reader knows how to make it long again.
